Chapter two. The Instruction Stack. How to make a model actually perform.

Here is the thing almost nobody wants to hear. The difference between the garbage a model hands you and the gold it can hand you is almost never the model. It is the instruction. You upgraded to the frontier tier. You are paying real money. And then you type one sentence, hit enter, and feel let down. The model is not the problem. The sentence is the problem.

Most people type a wish. "Write me a landing page." "Summarize this." "Make it better." A wish is a hope with no edges. You are handing a brilliant, literal, eager contractor a napkin with three words on it and walking away. Of course you get back something beige. You asked for nothing in particular, so it gave you the average of everything.

What you are going to learn instead is how to write a spec. Not a wish, a spec. And I want to be blunt about the math here. Getting good at writing the instruction is worth more to you than any model upgrade you will buy this year. A sharper operator on last quarter's model beats a sloppy operator on the newest one. Every time. The leverage is in your hands, on your keyboard, in the way you frame the job. So let's build the thing I call the Instruction Stack. Five layers. In order.

Layer one is role and spec. Who should the model be, and what exactly is the job. Not "you are a helpful assistant," that is wallpaper. Give it a real seat. "You are a conversion copywriter who has shipped pages for early stage software products." Then state the job in one clean line. "The job is to write the hero section for a tool that helps solo founders track expenses." Role plus job. The role narrows the model's whole personality toward the part of itself you actually want. The job tells it what done looks like. You would be amazed how many bad outputs trace straight back to the model not actually knowing what the deliverable was.

Layer two is context. This is the real situation. The documents. The data. What you have already tried. What you are reacting to. Here is where most people starve the model and then blame it for guessing. The model does not know your customer. It does not know that you tried the friendly tone last month and it flopped. It does not know your competitor's pricing unless you tell it. It does not know the weird constraint your business has that makes the obvious answer wrong. So tell it. Paste the actual thing. The real email. The real transcript. The real numbers. The real draft you hate. Stop describing your situation in the abstract and start handing over the artifacts. The model cannot read your mind, and it will not ask for the file you forgot to attach. It will just quietly fill the hole with a guess and hand it back to you wearing a confident face.

I want to camp on this for a second, because it is the single biggest lever in this whole chapter. The skill that matters most now is not clever prompt tricks. It is context engineering. A model with your context beats a smarter model without it. Read that again. A merely good model that knows your business, your voice, your constraints, your last three attempts, will smoke a genius model working blind. The genius working blind gives you a confident, generic answer. The good model with context gives you your answer. So the question stops being "how do I phrase this cleverly" and becomes "what does this thing need to know to do my job, and have I actually given it." That reframe is most of the game.

Layer three is constraints. The boundaries. The budget. The things you do not want. And critically, what the model should do when it is unsure. Here is a truth about these systems. An unconstrained model drifts to the beige average of everything it ever read. That is its center of gravity. Left alone, it will hand you the most expected, most middle, most forgettable version of the thing. Constraints are how you pull it off the average and onto your path. And here is the part that surprises people. Constraints do not lower quality by boxing the model in. They raise it. "Keep it under sixty words." "No exclamation points." "Do not use the word innovative or the word seamless." "Write like someone who is tired of marketing speak." Every fence you put up is a decision the model no longer has to average its way toward.

And negative constraints are the most underused tool you have. Telling the model what not to do is often more powerful than telling it what to do. "Do not open with a question." "Do not hedge." "Do not invent statistics." "Do not give me three options, give me your single best one." Negatives cut off whole regions of mediocre output in one stroke. Most people never write a single one. Start writing them.

There is one constraint that deserves special mention, because it saves you from the model's worst habit. Tell it what to do when it does not know. The default behavior, left unchecked, is to make something up and say it with total confidence. So put it in the instruction. "If you are not sure, say you are not sure. Do not invent facts, sources, or numbers. Flag anything you are guessing at." You are giving the model permission to be honest. Without that permission, it will choose to sound right over being right, every single time.

Layer four is the output contract. This is where you specify, precisely, the shape of the thing you want back. Vague format in, vague result out. If you say "give me some ideas," you will get a mushy paragraph and a shrug. If you say "give me exactly five ideas, each one a single sentence, ordered from safest to most aggressive," you get something you can actually use. Specify the artifact. How long. What structure. What it should and should not contain. What it looks like when it is done.

And the most powerful move inside the output contract is to show an example of good. Do not just describe the standard, demonstrate it. Paste a sample of the kind of output you want. "Here is a headline I think is strong, match this level of punch." Show, don't tell, applies to you instructing the machine just as much as it applies to writing. One concrete example of good is worth a paragraph of adjectives. And while you are at it, give a counter example. "Here is the kind of thing I do not want, never write like this." A model triangulates fast between a good example and a bad one. You are drawing the target instead of describing it.

Take the output contract one step further and hand the model a rubric. The actual checklist the work has to pass. "Before you give me the final version, check it against these. Is it under sixty words. Does it name the specific customer. Does it avoid the banned phrases. Does it make one clear promise." Then tell it to self check against that rubric before returning the answer. You are making the model grade its own homework before it turns it in. It catches its own misses. This one habit alone will lift your hit rate more than almost anything else.

Layer five is the eval loop. How you will check the output and feed corrections back. We will go deep on the full operating cycle in the next chapter, so I will keep this short. The point for now is that the instruction is not a one way broadcast. You set up from the start how you will judge what comes back and how you will tighten the next pass. Even something as simple as "after you answer, list the two weakest parts of your own response" turns a single shot into a loop. Build the checking into the instruction itself.

So that is the stack. Role and spec. Context. Constraints. Output contract. Eval loop. Now let me give you a few high leverage moves that sit on top of it.

The first one. For anything hard, make the model think before it answers. Models, like people, give worse answers when they blurt. The first words out are rarely the best words, but once they are out, everything after gets built on top of them. So slow it down on purpose. For a tough task, ask for a plan first. "Before you write anything, give me your plan of attack and wait." That way you can fix the approach before it has written three pages in the wrong direction. Or tell it to reason through the problem step by step before committing to an answer. Or, my favorite for anything ambiguous, tell it to ask you questions first. "Ask me three questions before you begin." That single line is a cheat code. The model will surface the exact ambiguities you did not realize you left open. You answer three questions, and the output goes from mediocre to sharp, because now it is solving your actual problem instead of a vague cousin of it. Thirty seconds of questions up front saves you three rounds of revision later.

Now let's talk about the failure modes you will hit, and the fix for each, because knowing the antidote is what separates an operator from a tourist.

First failure. Sycophancy. The model agreeing with you. You float a half baked idea and it tells you it is brilliant. It is not lying exactly, it is just built to be agreeable, and agreeable is not useful when you need the truth. The fix is to force the other side. "Argue against this idea." "Give me the three strongest reasons this fails." "Critique this as if you were the person who has to clean up the mess." When you ask the model to attack your idea, you get the honest read you actually needed. Make it your sparring partner, not your fan.

Second failure. Confident hallucination. The model states something false with the exact same tone it uses for things that are true. This is the dangerous one, because it sounds identical to a correct answer. The fix lives in your constraints, but say it explicitly. Require sources. "Cite where this comes from, and if you cannot, mark it as unverified." Give it the out of saying "I don't know." A model that is allowed to say I don't know will use it. A model that is not allowed will fill the gap with fiction.

Third failure. The model answers an easier, nearby question instead of yours. You ask something specific and three sided, and you get a smooth answer to a simpler question you did not ask. The fix is to restate the exact question and require it address each part. "Answer these three things separately, label each one, and do not skip any." Pin it down. Make the structure of your question into the structure of its answer.

Now, one expectation I want to reset. Your first prompt is usually not the finished instruction. It is a probe. It is a tool for discovering what you actually want. You will read the first output and think, oh, that is not it, but now I know why, because that is too formal, or it missed the audience, or it buried the point. Good. That is the prompt doing its job. It showed you your own preferences by violating them. So you tighten and rerun. Add the constraint you just discovered. Paste the example you now know you need. Iteration is not failure. It is the method. Anyone who tells you they nail it in one shot is either lucky, lying, or working on something trivial.

So let me land this. Instructing a model well is a writing skill and a thinking skill. It is being able to say clearly what you want, what good looks like, and what to avoid. Those are deeply human skills. They are also deeply learnable. Nobody is born knowing how to write a spec. You build it, rep by rep, and unlike chasing the latest model, this is leverage that compounds. Every sharp instruction you write makes the next one sharper.

And here is the bridge to what comes next. Once you can write one great instruction, the obvious question is how you run them, repeatably, for real deep work, without reinventing the wheel every time. That is a cycle. I call it the Jaystay Loop. Frame, brief, constrain, unleash, inspect, bank. That is the next chapter. The Instruction Stack is how you write a great instruction. The Loop is how you turn it into a machine. Let's go.
