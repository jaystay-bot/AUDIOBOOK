Chapter five. Build with what's free. Open repos, public endpoints, and shipping a real product.

Let's start with the good news, because it's almost unfair. Right now, the internet is overflowing with free, powerful parts. There are open-source repositories that took skilled teams years to write, sitting there for you to use. There are open-weight models you can download and run yourself. There are public APIs and free tiers that give you capabilities that used to cost a fortune. There are open datasets, ready-made components, whole pieces of infrastructure given away for nothing. The raw materials of a serious software product are lying around like driftwood on a beach.

So here's the thing you need to internalize. The bottleneck is no longer access to parts. That war is over, and you won. The bottleneck now is judgment. It's the ability to look at ten thousand free parts and know which three to assemble into something a real person will actually pay for. Most people never get there. They drown in parts. They collect repositories like trophies, they star things they'll never open again, they bookmark APIs, and they ship absolutely nothing. Abundance without judgment is just noise. Your job in this chapter is to become the person who turns the pile of free parts into a product.

Let's get concrete, because the listener asked a real question. How do you actually use an open-source repository properly? Before you build anything on top of someone's code, you evaluate it like a grown-up. And there are two completely different questions hiding inside "should I use this." The first is, can I legally take this. The second is, should I technically build on this. Don't confuse them.

Start with "should I build on this," the technical question. Is the project alive? Open it up and look at the activity. Are there recent commits, or did the last one land two years ago? Are maintainers responding to issues, or is it a graveyard of unanswered questions? Are real bugs getting closed? A live project means that when something breaks, there's a human on the other end. A dead one means you just adopted an orphan.

Next, is it actually used? Be careful here, because stars are a weak signal. Stars are how developers say "neat," not "I depend on this." A repository can have forty thousand stars and almost no real usage. What you want is evidence of adoption. How many downloads does the package get? How many other projects depend on it? Is anyone you've heard of using it in production? Real adoption means real testing, means the sharp edges have been found by someone other than you.

Then, is the documentation real? Not a pretty landing page. Real docs that explain how the thing works and how to use it. And the acid test: can you get it running in about ten minutes? Clone it, follow the quick start, and see if you reach a working example fast. If you can't get "hello world" out of it in ten minutes with decent docs, imagine what the hard parts will feel like. A project that respects your time at the front door usually respects it everywhere.

Now the other question, the one people skip and later regret. The license. This is the "can I take it" question, and you answer it by reading, not by guessing. Let me put licenses in plain English, because the legalese scares people off and that's exactly how they get burned.

There are two big families. Permissive licenses and copyleft licenses. The permissive ones, names like M.I.T., Apache, and B.S.D., are the friendly ones. They basically say: use this, build a commercial product on it, keep your own code private, just keep the copyright notice around. Apache adds one more nice thing, an explicit patent grant, which means the contributors won't turn around and sue you over patents in the code they gave you. For a solo builder shipping a paid product, permissive licenses are green lights. Take them, build, sell.

Then there's copyleft, names like G.P.L. and especially A.G.P.L. These licenses come with a string attached, and the string is sharp. Copyleft says: if you use this and distribute your product, you have to open-source your product too, under the same terms. And A.G.P.L. goes further, which is the part that traps people. A.G.P.L. triggers not just when you distribute software, but when you let people use it over a network. So if you build a closed, paid web service on top of A.G.P.L. code, the license can require you to open-source your whole service to your users. For a private software-as-a-service business, A.G.P.L. is a landmine unless you genuinely know what you're doing. I'm not telling you copyleft is evil. It exists for good reasons, to keep open things open. I'm telling you to know which one you're standing on before you pour concrete.

So the rule is simple. Always check the license before you fall in love with the repo. Reading the license takes two minutes. Ripping a copyleft component out of your product after you've built your business around it takes two months and a lawyer.

Now, even when the license is clean and the project is alive, how you use the code matters. Don't fork blindly. The instinct of a beginner is to copy the code into their own project so they "own" it. Usually that's a mistake. Prefer depending on a maintained library over copying its source. When you depend on it, you get the bug fixes and security patches for free, forever. When you copy it, you've adopted a snapshot that starts rotting the moment you paste it. Copy only when you have a real reason, and know that you've signed up to maintain it.

And here's a line I want you to carry out of this chapter. Read enough of the source to understand the load-bearing parts. You don't have to understand every file. But the pieces your product leans on, the parts that handle your money, your data, your security, your core logic, you understand those. This matters double now, because you're going to have agents writing your integration code. Never ship code you don't understand in the parts that matter. Not code you wrote, not code an agent wrote. If an agent wires up your payment flow and you can't explain how it works, you don't have a product. You have a liability with a nice front end.

Let's talk about public endpoints and public APIs, because that was the other half of the question, and it's where good citizenship and self-interest line up perfectly.

First, distinguish two very different things. There's an official, documented A.P.I., where the provider has published an interface and is inviting you to use it. That's a contract. You can build on it. And then there's scraping, where you point code at a website that never agreed to be a data source and yank content out of the page. Scraping is fragile, it breaks the moment they change their layout, and it's often flat-out against their terms of service. If there's an official A.P.I., use it. If you're scraping, know that you're building on sand.

When you do use an A.P.I., use it properly. Read the terms of service and the rate limits. Authenticate with a proper key instead of sneaking in the side door. Respect the rate limits, and when you get throttled, back off gracefully instead of hammering harder. Cache responses, so you're not asking someone's free endpoint the same question a thousand times an hour. A free endpoint is usually someone's generosity or someone's hobby. Treat it the way you'd treat a friend who's doing you a favor. Don't abuse the favor.

And two hard security rules that have no exceptions. Never hardcode secrets in client code. Never commit a key to a repository. The moment an A.P.I. key lands in your front-end code or your public repo, assume it's compromised, because bots scan for exactly that, around the clock. Keys live in environment variables and secret managers on the server side, never in anything a user or a search engine can see.

One more piece of judgment about free endpoints. Assume any free thing can disappear or start charging tomorrow. The hobby project that powers your killer feature can go dark on a random Tuesday because the maintainer got a job and stopped paying the hosting bill. So don't build your entire business on a single unpaid endpoint with no fallback. Either pay for reliability where it's load-bearing, or design so you can swap providers when one vanishes. Free is a wonderful way to start. It is a terrible single point of failure.

So step back and look at what you're really doing here, because there's a mindset that ties all of this together. You are a composer, not an inventor. You are not writing a database from scratch, or training a model from zero, or inventing a new image format. A real product in 2026 is usually this: an open-source component, plus a model A.P.I., plus a public data source, plus your specific glue, plus your specific insight about a specific customer. Look at that list. The parts are commodities. Anyone can grab them. The glue and the insight are yours. That's the whole game. Your value is not the components, because the components are free to everyone. Your value is knowing how to assemble them and knowing which customer's pain they solve. And the integration grunt work, the wiring, the plumbing, the boring connective code, that's exactly what you hand to your agents, with the guardrails from the last chapter, budgets and dry-runs and checkpoints, so they do the labor and you keep the judgment.

Now the part everyone gets backwards. The path from repo to product does not start with the repo. It starts with the customer's problem. The classic trap, the one that has buried a thousand smart builders, is finding a cool piece of technology and then going looking for a problem it might solve. You build the tech you found instead of the thing someone needs. Flip it. Start with a real person and a real problem. Then go shopping in the free aisle for parts that solve it.

Once you have the problem, make the smallest thing that delivers the value end to end. Not the most impressive demo. The smallest complete loop where a real person gets a real result. And then, this is where your money is, wrap the free parts in something a normal human finds easy and trustworthy. Because here's the uncomfortable truth: the open-source library at the heart of your product is available to your customer for free. They could get it. They just can't, or won't, assemble it, host it, secure it, and babysit it. You're not selling the library. You're selling the solved problem and the experience around it. Your moat is the assembly, the polish, the distribution, and the trust. Those are the things free parts can't give your customer, and those are the things you provide.

A quick word on being a good citizen, because it's right and because it pays. Credit the projects you build on. Respect the licenses, actually respect them, not just technically. And when you find a bug and fix it, contribute the fix back upstream. It costs you a pull request and it buys you relationships with maintainers, a reputation as someone who gives, and goodwill in a community that can help you later. Open source ran on that bargain for decades. Honor it.

Let me name the pitfalls plainly so you can see them coming. License violations that seem fine today and blow up the day you get acquired and someone reads your dependencies. Depending on one free endpoint with no fallback, then watching your product die when it does. Shipping a pile of A.I.-generated integration code that nobody on earth understands, including you. Building the demo over and over and never building the actual product. And the security ones: leaking keys, and trusting untrusted data as if it were safe. Every one of these is avoidable with the discipline we just walked through.

So here's where we land. The parts are free, and they're effectively infinite, and that's not your problem to solve. The value was never in the parts. The value is in knowing which problem is worth solving, choosing the right pieces, understanding the load-bearing ones, and assembling a whole that a real person can trust. The parts are commodities. The assembly is the craft. That judgment, what to point all this free power at, and how to make it trustworthy, is the human edge. And it's exactly that edge that the last chapter is about. Because if the parts are free and the building is getting cheap, there's one question left that decides everything. In a world like that, what makes you hard to replace. That's where we go next.
