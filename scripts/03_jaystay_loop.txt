Chapter three. The Jaystay Loop. A repeatable cycle for getting deep work out of a machine.

Up to now we've talked about the territory and about how to write a single good instruction. Now I want to give you the thing you'll actually use every day. Because real work isn't one prompt. Real work is a process. And if you don't have a process, you end up doing the thing almost everyone does, which is chat. You type, it answers, you type again, it answers again, and three hours later you have a long conversation and nothing you'd be proud to ship. Chatting feels like progress. It usually isn't. What you want is a loop. A loop has a beginning, an end, and a result you can keep.

So here's the loop. Six moves. Frame, brief, constrain, unleash, inspect, bank. I call it the Jaystay Loop because it's the operating cycle, the thing you run on repeat. Let me walk you through each move, slowly, because the magic is in the details and the order.

Move one. Frame. Before you tell the machine anything, you decide what done looks like. Not what to do. What done looks like. These are completely different questions and confusing them is the single most common mistake people make. If I ask a model to research the market for a product, that's a task with no edge, and it'll happily generate pages of plausible nothing forever. But if I say, by the end I want a one-page memo that answers three specific questions, names the five biggest competitors with their pricing, and ends with a clear go or no-go recommendation, now there's a shape. Now the work has a target. Framing is you, the human, doing the part the machine cannot do. You are deciding what matters. Spend real time here. Sixty seconds of framing saves an hour of wandering. If you can't describe what done looks like, you are not ready to start, and no model on earth can rescue you from a goal you haven't set.

Move two. Brief. Now you load the context. This is where you give the machine everything it needs to actually do the job, and it's where most people starve their model and then blame the model. The machine doesn't know your situation. It doesn't know your customer, your constraints, your past attempts, the three things you already tried that didn't work, the weird internal reason option B is off the table. A good brief front-loads all of that. Who is this for. What's the background. What have we already ruled out and why. What does good look like, with an example if you have one. What's the format of the answer. Here is a rule that will serve you forever. A model with your context will beat a smarter model without it, almost every time. The intelligence is rented and cheap. The context is yours and it's the whole edge. So pour it in. Paste the real documents. Show it the actual code, the actual numbers, the actual messy reality. A vague brief gets a vague result, and that's not the machine failing, that's you under-briefing.

Move three. Constrain. Now you draw the fences. Constraints are not the enemy of good work, they're the cause of it. An unconstrained model drifts toward the safe, average, beige middle of everything it's ever read, because that's what it was trained to do. Your constraints are what pull it off the beige path and onto your path. So you tell it the boundaries. Don't suggest anything that requires more than two people to run. Stay under this budget. We are not changing the database, work within what exists. Use only these sources, and if you're not sure, say so instead of guessing. Keep it under a page. Match the style of this example. Every real constraint you add narrows the space and raises the quality, because you've taken the infinite ocean of okay answers and fenced it down to the small pond of answers that actually fit your life. And there's a hidden constraint people forget, which is what to do when stuck. Tell it. If you hit something you can't verify, stop and ask, don't invent. That one line prevents most of the confident nonsense people complain about.

Move four. Unleash. Now, and only now, you let it run. You've framed the target, loaded the context, drawn the fences. Let it go and resist the urge to micromanage every step. This is the move people are worst at, in two opposite directions. Some people never unleash. They keep such a tight leash that the model can't take more than one tiny step before they interrupt, and they get none of the leverage, they're just doing the work themselves with extra steps. Other people unleash into the void with no frame and no fences and then act shocked when it runs off a cliff. The skill is unleashing into structure. Inside a good frame and good fences, a model can do an astonishing amount in one go. Let it produce the whole memo. Let it draft all five sections. Let it write the first version of the entire thing. You'll inspect it in a second. But give it room to actually do the work, because a model interrupted every thirty seconds never builds momentum, and momentum is where the good stuff comes from. Unleash means trust the structure you built, not trust the machine blindly. You're not trusting it. You're trusting your frame.

Move five. Inspect. Now you put your operator hat on, the most important hat you own. The output is back. Do not skim it and say looks good. That sentence, looks good, has shipped more garbage into the world than any bug ever has. Inspect like you mean it. And here's the key, you're not checking whether it sounds good, you're checking whether it's true and whether it's done. Sounding good is exactly what these systems are best at and exactly what will fool you. So go to the load-bearing parts. Did it actually answer the three questions from your frame, or did it answer easier questions nearby. Are the facts real. Pick a claim and verify it yourself, especially the ones you want to be true, because those are the ones that get past you. Did it follow the constraints or quietly ignore the budget. Is the recommendation actually supported by what came before it or did it just assert a confident ending. Inspection is where your judgment, your taste, your experience all earn their keep. This is the part the machine genuinely cannot do for itself, because a model grading its own work is just a second guess wearing a confident voice. You are the ground truth. Be a hard grader. Hard grading at this step is the entire difference between someone who produces a lot of impressive-looking nothing and someone who produces things that hold up.

And inspection feeds back. If it's wrong, you don't just say fix it and pray. You say exactly what's wrong and why, you tighten the frame or add the missing context or draw a fence you forgot, and you run the loop again. That's the loop closing. Most work takes two or three turns of the loop, not one, and each turn is sharper because you learned something about what you actually wanted. The first pass is often really a tool for discovering your own requirements. That's fine. That's the process working.

Move six. Bank. This is the move almost everyone skips, and skipping it is why people feel like they start from zero every single day. When something works, you save it. The good brief that got a great result, save it, it's a template now. The set of constraints that finally got the tone right, save it. The framing that made the model click into the actual problem, that's reusable, bank it. Over a few months of doing this you build a personal library of instructions that work, and that library is worth more than any single output it ever produced. This is how you compound. The person who banks gets better every week because every win becomes a tool. The person who doesn't bank is doing the same cold start forever, equally amazed and equally stuck every morning, wondering why it never gets easier. Bank your wins. Banking is the difference between learning and just experiencing.

So that's the loop. Frame, brief, constrain, unleash, inspect, bank. Let me make it concrete so it's not abstract.

Say you want to build a landing page for an idea. The amateur move is, hey, build me a landing page, and you take whatever comes. The operator runs the loop. Frame. Done looks like a single page that makes one specific promise to one specific person and has one clear call to action, and I'll know it's good if a stranger can tell me in one sentence what it's for. Brief. Here's the product, here's exactly who it's for, here are three competitors whose pages I like and the one thing I like about each, here's my actual rough copy, here's the brand feel in three words. Constrain. One page, no signup wall, loads fast, works on a phone first, no stock-photo clichés, use only these brand colors. Unleash. Build the whole thing, all sections, real copy not placeholder. Inspect. Does it make one promise or five. Would the specific person it's for actually get it in three seconds. Is the call to action obvious or buried. Then fix exactly what's off and run again. Bank. That brief and those constraints are now your landing-page recipe, and the next one takes ten minutes instead of an afternoon. Same six moves, every time, whether it's a landing page, a market memo, a chunk of code, or a plan for your week.

Now let me give you the deeper reason this works, because I don't want you to just memorize six words. The loop works because it puts the human exactly where the human belongs and the machine exactly where the machine belongs. Look at who does what. Framing is you. Briefing is mostly you, supplying judgment about what matters. Constraining is you, your taste and your boundaries. Inspecting is you, your standard of truth. Banking is you, your learning. The machine's job is one move out of six. Unleash. The doing. The grinding production of the actual draft. That's it. That's the right division of labor, and it tells you something profound about this whole moment. You are not being replaced by the loop, you are being concentrated into the parts of it that are most you. The machine takes the typing. You keep the deciding. And the more of these systems' raw power grows, the more valuable the deciding becomes, not less, because a more powerful engine pointed badly just makes a bigger crater.

People who feel out-competed by AI are almost always stuck on move four. They think the whole game is the doing, the production, the output, and the machine is obviously better at that, so they feel doomed. But the doing was never the valuable part. It was just the visible part. The valuable part was always the framing and the judgment, and that was invisible, so we never named it or charged for it. This loop names it. It makes the invisible work the explicit work. And once you see it that way, the fear flips into something useful, because now you know exactly what to get good at.

So here's your assignment, and please actually do it, don't just nod. Take one real thing you need to get done this week, something that matters, and run all six moves on it deliberately, out loud in your head. Write the frame down before you type a single prompt. Build a real brief. Set actual constraints. Then unleash, then inspect like a hard grader, then bank what worked. It'll feel slow the first time, the way using a checklist feels slow until the day it saves you. Do it five times and it stops being a checklist and becomes how you think. That's the goal. Not to remember the loop, but to become it.

In the next chapter we take this further, into the deep end, where the machine runs for a long time on its own and you're not watching every move. Because once you trust the loop, the natural next question is, how do I let it run without me, safely, for real work. That's autonomy inside guardrails, and it's where this stops being a tool you use and becomes a team you run.
